Application to assistive navigation

Listen to this page

From a depth map to a spoken warning

This page puts the pieces together into the design of a phone based navigation aid. Each frame passes through these stages:

  1. Capture the frame, and read the phone's gravity sensor to know how the camera is tilted.
  2. Predict metric depth and relative uncertainty (Parts 4 and 5).
  3. Split the image into left, center and right zones, and ignore pixels that belong to the floor.
  4. Find the nearest depth in each zone, using a low percentile rather than the minimum so that one noisy pixel cannot trigger an alarm.
  5. Smooth those distances over time (Part 7).
  6. Decide what to say, if anything, and say it by voice or vibration.

Prioritizing nearby hazards

The center zone is the user's walking corridor and is always checked first. Side zones only matter when they are very close or when the system suggests a direction. A useful way to rank hazards is time to contact: distance divided by walking speed. At a typical speed of about 1.2 meters per second, an obstacle 1.5 meters ahead is little more than one second away, which is roughly the limit for a spoken warning to be useful.

Move the sliders to see how the system from Part 5 would respond to different distances and uncertainty levels. Notice how a high uncertainty overrides even a "safe looking" distance.

Comparison with other sensing approaches

Monocular depth is not the only option. The table compares it with sensors commonly considered for assistive devices.

ApproachCostRangeGlassMain limitation
Monocular depth (this tutorial)None beyond the phoneWhole sceneOften failsEstimated, not measured
Phone LiDARHigh end phones onlyAbout 5 mOften failsLimited phones, low resolution
Stereo cameraExtra hardwareGrows with camera spacingOften failsWeak on plain walls
Ultrasonic sensorCheapA few meters, one directionDetects itNo scene detail

The interesting point is that glass defeats most optical sensors, while a cheap ultrasonic sensor detects it. Combining monocular depth for scene detail with an ultrasonic sensor for the straight ahead check is, in my view, the most realistic low cost design.

Safety issues when the prediction is wrong

The device should complement the cane, never replace it, and should say so during setup. Warnings must be short and predictable. And the "uncertain" message must be treated as a normal output, tested with users, rather than as an error message.
