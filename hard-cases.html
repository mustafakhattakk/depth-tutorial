Difficult real world conditions

Listen to this page

Benchmarks are dominated by tidy rooms and sunny streets. Real walks are not. Recent challenge results [16] show that even the best current models still lose accuracy on difficult scenes. This page goes through the conditions that matter most for a pedestrian, why each one breaks depth estimation, and what can be done about it.

Transparent objects such as glass doors

A glass door shows the room behind it. A depth model sees the far wall and reports its distance, so the door is invisible. This is the most dangerous failure for navigation because it produces a confident "clear" exactly where a collision is likely. Even the training labels are wrong here: Kinect and laser sensors also pass through glass. Costanzino and colleagues tackle this by painting glass regions with plain texture so a depth model treats them as solid, using those predictions as new labels, and fine tuning on them [17].

Mirrors and reflective surfaces

A mirror shows a virtual scene that appears to be behind the wall. The model happily estimates depth for the reflected room. Shiny floors cause a similar effect and can make the floor look like a hole. Mirrors are treated with the same labeling approach as glass [17], and frame borders and door handles give the model clues that a solid surface exists.

Poor or changing lighting

In dim light the camera adds noise and blur, and many depth cues disappear. Bright windows cause the camera to darken the rest of the image. Uncertainty from relighted copies of the image (Part 5) catches many of these cases.

Small or thin obstacles

Poles, chair legs, cables and table edges occupy few pixels, and decoders tend to smooth them into the background. Depth Anything V2 is noticeably sharper here than older models [9], and Depth Pro was designed for sharp boundaries [11], but thin objects at a distance remain unreliable.

Partially hidden objects and unusual camera angles

Training photos are mostly taken at eye level, held straight. A phone hanging around a user's neck or pointed at the floor creates tilted views where the usual cues such as the horizon and floor lines are missing or strange. Occluded objects are usually still placed correctly in order, but their size cues are incomplete.

Indoor versus outdoor and unfamiliar scenes

Indoor depths are mostly under ten meters while outdoor scenes reach hundreds. A metric model tuned for one range is often biased in the other, which is why ZoeDepth routes between indoor and outdoor heads [10] and Depth Anything V2 ships separate metric models [9]. Scenes far from any training data, such as construction zones or crowded markets, raise epistemic uncertainty.
