Monocular depth estimation models

How a network turns an image into a depth map

Nearly all depth networks have an encoder and a decoder. The encoder shrinks the image into compact features that capture context, such as "this is a hallway and that region is a floor." The decoder grows those features back to full resolution and outputs one depth value per pixel, usually with skip connections that pass fine detail straight from the encoder. The difference between models lies in the encoder, the training data and the loss function.

MiDaS: robustness through mixed data

MiDaS [3] made two contributions that shaped the field. The first is the scale and shift invariant loss. For every training image, the prediction is first aligned to the ground truth with the best possible scale and shift, and only the remaining error is penalized:

L_ssi = (1 / 2M) Σ_i | (s·d_i + t) − d*_i | with (s, t) = argmin over s, t of Σ_i (s·d_i + t − d*_i)²

Because the alignment absorbs differences in units, datasets with incompatible scales can be mixed. The second contribution is the evaluation protocol: test on datasets never used in training. This zero shot transfer test is much closer to real deployment than reporting accuracy on the training dataset's own test split.

DPT: transformers for dense prediction

DPT [7] replaced the convolutional encoder with a vision transformer. The image is cut into 16 by 16 pixel patches, and every transformer layer lets every patch attend to every other patch. A convolutional network only sees a growing local window, so it can miss the global layout. DPT reassembles features from several transformer layers into maps at different resolutions and fuses them in a convolutional decoder. The result is depth that is more consistent across the whole image, and newer MiDaS releases adopted it.

Depth Anything: scale through unlabeled data

Depth Anything [8] asked a different question: what if the bottleneck is data rather than architecture? The authors trained a teacher model on about 1.5 million labeled images, used it to label about 62 million unlabeled images, and trained a student on all of it. Two details made this work: the student had to predict depth on strongly distorted copies of images (color changes, cut and paste mixing), and its features were kept close to those of a frozen DINOv2 encoder, which carries rich semantic knowledge about objects.

Depth Anything V2: synthetic labels, real images

Depth Anything V2 [9] made one bold change. Real depth labels from sensors are noisy, have holes, and are often wrong on glass and thin structures. The authors therefore trained the teacher only on about 595 thousand synthetic images rendered with perfect depth. Synthetic images look different from real ones, so the teacher then labeled about 62 million real images, and students were trained on those pseudo labels. The released students range from about 25 million parameters (Small) to about 335 million (Large), with a 1.3 billion parameter Giant teacher. The authors also released metric versions fine tuned on Hypersim for indoor scenes and Virtual KITTI for outdoor scenes.

CNN encoders (early MiDaS, FastDepth)Transformer encoders (DPT, Depth Anything)
ContextLocal, grows with depth of networkGlobal from the first layer

Generalization to unseen images

For an assistive tool, every user's home, office and street is a scene the model has never seen. In my view, zero shot robustness matters more here than the best score on a single benchmark. That is the main reason I focus on Depth Anything V2 in the rest of this tutorial: it was designed around generalization, it comes in a size that can run on a phone, and it has metric versions.
